Main Text Figures

Figure 1
fig_01_mpa_map.pdf
Figure 1. Map of 23 marine protected areas (MPAs) along the California coast included in the analysis. Points indicate MPA locations; color indicates data source (KFM/NPS-MBON, SBC-LTER, PISCO, or Landsat). Bathymetric shading shows the continental shelf. All MPAs were established between 2003 and 2012 as part of California's Marine Life Protection Act.
Figure 2
fig_02_cascade_case_studies.pdf
Figure 2. Trophic cascade case studies at representative MPAs. Each column shows a single MPA; rows show predators (Panulirus interruptus, Semicossyphus pulcher), prey (Strongylocentrotus purpuratus, Mesocentrotus franciscanus), and kelp (Macrocystis pyrifera). Points show log response ratios (lnRR = ln[MPA/Reference]) over time; vertical dashed lines mark MPA implementation. The predicted trophic cascade pattern is: predators increase (positive lnRR), urchins decrease (negative lnRR), and kelp increases (positive lnRR).
Figure 3
fig_03_mean_effects.pdf
Figure 3. Meta-analytic mean effects of MPA protection on kelp forest taxa. Points show estimated mean log response ratios from the joint multilevel meta-analysis (metafor::rma.mv); horizontal bars show 95% confidence intervals. The x-axis is displayed on the back-transformed response ratio (RR) scale, where RR = 1 (dashed line) indicates no MPA effect. Filled points indicate effects significant after Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction (p < 0.05); open points are non-significant. Numbers in parentheses show the number of MPA-level effect sizes (k) for each taxon-response combination.
Figure 4
fig_04_recovery_curves.pdf
Figure 4. Temporal recovery trajectories of MPA effects on kelp forest biomass. Panels show predators (P. interruptus, S. pulcher), urchins (S. purpuratus, M. franciscanus), and kelp (M. pyrifera). Thin colored lines show individual MPA trajectories (lnRR over years since MPA establishment); thick lines show population-level linear mixed effects model (lmer) predictions with 95% confidence bands. Positive slopes indicate increasing MPA effects over time. Temporal models used the specification: lnRR ~ time × species + (1 + time | MPA) + (1 | source).

Figure S15
fig_s15_sensitivity_summary.pdf
Figure S15. Cook's distance and outlier sensitivity comparison across four analytical approaches: (1) joint model without outlier removal (primary), (2) per-taxa models without removal, (3) per-taxa Cook's D at 4/k threshold, and (4) joint Cook's D at 4/n. The primary analysis (no removal) is preferred because the global Cook's D threshold over-flags between-taxon differences as outliers (see Section 2.4 of Analysis Revisions).
Figure S16
fig_s16_outlier_rationale.pdf
Figure S16. Outlier removal rationale. Four-panel visualization showing why global Cook's D thresholds over-flag observations in the joint meta-analysis. Between-taxon divergence in effect sizes (the expected trophic cascade signal) drives most flagged observations, not within-taxon anomalies. Per-taxon Cook's D (4/k) flagged only 11 of 132 observations compared to 88 of 132 under the global threshold (4/n).
Figure S17
fig_s17_temporal_outlier_trajectories.pdf
Figure S17. Temporal trajectories (lnRR over time since MPA establishment) for observations flagged as outliers by the global Cook's D threshold. Highlighted observations follow species-typical temporal patterns rather than showing anomalous behavior, supporting the decision to retain the full dataset in the primary analysis.
Figure S18
fig_s18_raw_trajectories_outlier_status.pdf
Figure S18. Raw data trajectories colored by outlier status under the global Cook's D threshold (4/n). Panels are faceted by taxa and response type. Flagged observations (red) are concentrated in taxa with large between-group effect size differences, confirming that global thresholds conflate biological signal with statistical influence.
